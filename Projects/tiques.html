PROJECT OVERVIEW

Exploring a potential climate–vector relationship

Tick species are sensitive to environmental conditions that influence their activity, survival and geographic distribution. Changes in climate conditions may therefore contribute to changes in the areas where vector species are observed.

This project investigates whether changes in relative humidity between a historical reference period and 2017–2023 are associated with the appearance of five tick species in new European regions.

02

RESEARCH QUESTION

Can changes in relative humidity help explain the emergence of tick species?

The analysis focuses on five tick species occurring across different parts of Europe: Dermacentor reticulatus, Hyalomma marginatum, Ixodes persulcatus, Ixodes ricinus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus.

The central hypothesis was that changes in relative humidity could contribute to the appearance of species in regions where they had not previously been reported.

The study therefore combines a spatial comparison of climate and species distribution with a statistical analysis designed to test whether humidity change is associated with newly reported presence.

Importantly, the analysis is exploratory: tick distribution is influenced by multiple environmental and ecological drivers, so relative humidity cannot be interpreted in isolation.

DATA

Combining climate and species-distribution data

Relative humidity data from NASA's AIRS programme were downloaded as NetCDF files and processed in R. Daytime and nighttime observations were aggregated to obtain monthly and annual averages.

Tick distribution data were obtained from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and represented species presence across European administrative regions.

Time series on the humidity per european district.

Climate rasters were spatially integrated with NUTS2 administrative regions, allowing the humidity signal to be compared with changes in reported species presence.

The climate analysis covered two periods: a 2003–2016 reference period and a 2017–2023 study period.

DATA PROCESSING

From NetCDF climate data to regional indicators

The downloaded NetCDF datasets did not contain an explicit coordinate reference system. The WGS84 geographic reference provided by the data source was therefore integrated before spatial processing.

Daytime and nighttime relative humidity values were averaged monthly, then aggregated to annual values. These annual datasets were subsequently aggregated over the study periods.

CLIMATE PATTERNS

Relative humidity varies substantially across Europe

Between 2017 and 2023, mean relative humidity exceeded approximately 60% across much of northern Europe, while values generally ranged between 45% and 60% across central and southern Europe.

The spatial pattern provides an environmental baseline for examining whether regions with newly reported tick presence experienced comparable changes in humidity.

Difference in mean relative humidity between the study period (2017–2023) and the reference period (2003–2016).

Relative humidity increased in parts of northern Europe, the Alps and Mediterranean coastal areas.

Decreases were observed across parts of central Europe, Spain and Portugal.

TICK DISTRIBUTION

Five species show different spatial patterns

The five species occupy distinct geographical ranges. Some are concentrated in northern Europe, others around the Mediterranean, while Dermacentor reticulatus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus show broader distributions.

The number of newly affected regions remained relatively limited for most species, although Hyalomma marginatum showed a substantially larger increase in reported presence.

Distribution of the five analysed tick species across Europe, 2017–2023.

Dermacentor reticulatus was newly reported in 34 regions, while Hyalomma marginatum was reported in 121 additional regions.

Ixodes persulcatus, Ixodes ricinus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus showed smaller increases, with 7, 14 and 21 additional regions respectively.

SPATIAL COMPARISON

Newly reported tick presence does not follow a simple humidity pattern

A visual comparison between the tick-distribution maps and humidity anomalies reveals no consistent spatial pattern across all species.

For example, some newly reported regions of Ixodes persulcatus coincide with relatively large humidity increases, while other species appear in regions where relative humidity decreased.

Dermacentor reticulatus illustrates this contrast particularly well: newly reported regions occur both in parts of Spain and Portugal, where humidity decreased, and in Italy, where humidity increased.

Similar contrasts can be observed for Hyalomma marginatum and Rhipicephalus sanguineus, suggesting that relative humidity alone cannot readily explain the observed spatial changes.

LOGISTIC REGRESSION

Testing the climate–distribution relationship

Logistic regression was used to test whether changes in relative humidity were associated with newly reported tick presence across European regions.

The initial model compared the 2017–2023 study period with a 2003–2016 reference period.

REFERENCE PERIOD SENSITIVITY

Results depend strongly on the temporal baseline

A second analysis used 2017 as the reference year instead of the 2003–2016 climatological baseline.

The resulting coefficients and statistical evidence differed substantially, particularly for Ixodes ricinus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus.

Relative humidity difference using 2017 as the reference year.

This sensitivity to the reference period is an important methodological result: the estimated climate–distribution relationship can change depending on how the baseline is defined.

The longer 2003–2016 baseline provides a more stable climatological reference, but the uncertainty surrounding the actual arrival date of tick species limits the interpretation of both approaches.

10

LIMITATIONS

Interpreting the results requires caution

Tick observations were qualitative: the dataset indicates whether a species was reported in a region, but does not quantify abundance, observation effort or detection probability.

In addition, regions classified as “not reported” could not necessarily be interpreted as true absence. This introduces uncertainty into the binary response used by the regression.

The use of administrative regions also introduces a potential Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP). Tick movement occurs at much finer spatial scales through hosts and suitable habitats.

Finally, tick distribution is multifactorial. Temperature, winter conditions, vegetation, soil moisture and host availability may all contribute to distribution changes.

KEY FINDINGS

What the analysis shows

01 — Tick distributions changed between 2017 and 2023

All five species showed some newly reported presence, although the magnitude of change varied strongly between species.

02 — No consistent humidity pattern emerged

Newly reported regions occurred in areas where relative humidity both increased and decreased.

03 — The statistical evidence was inconsistent

The first logistic regression did not provide consistent evidence of an association between humidity change and newly reported tick presence.

04 — The reference period matters

Changing the temporal baseline substantially modified the estimated relationships for some species.

05 — Data limitations are central

Qualitative observations, uncertain arrival dates and coarse spatial units limit the strength of the inference.

06 — A broader climate-health framework is needed

Future analyses should integrate humidity with temperature, vegetation, soil moisture and host dynamics rather than treating relative humidity as an isolated driver.

12

NEXT STEPS

Towards finer-scale climate–health modelling

A future version of the analysis could replace relative humidity with saturation deficit, which provides a more directly relevant measure of atmospheric moisture availability for tick ecology.

Climate variables could then be combined with temperature, vegetation and land-cover data within a multivariate modelling framework.

Using georeferenced point observations rather than administrative presence/absence data would also reduce the effects of spatial aggregation and allow the analysis to operate at a more ecologically meaningful scale.

These improvements would provide a stronger framework for investigating how environmental change may influence the spatial dynamics of vector-borne disease risk.
